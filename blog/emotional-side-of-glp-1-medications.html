The Emotional Side of GLP-1 Medications: Body Image, Identity, and Your Relationship With Food

By Tracy Croghan, LICSW Published November 18, 2026 8 minute read
The short answer

Some people taking GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic, Wegovy, and Zepbound notice emotional changes alongside the physical ones. These can include shifts in body image and identity, a changed relationship with food, strain in relationships, and sometimes changes in mood. Therapy can help you work through all of this. It does not replace your prescriber, who is the right person for questions about dosing, side effects, or whether to continue a medication.

Why the emotional side matters

GLP-1 medications have gone from a niche prescription to a household word. A 2024 KFF poll, reported by the American Psychological Association, found that about 12% of U.S. adults have used one, and roughly four in ten of those users took it mainly for weight loss.

Most conversations about these medications focus on dosing, side effects, and the number on the scale. Far fewer make room for the question I hear most from people who are on them: how am I supposed to feel about all of this? That question deserves a real answer.

Emotional changes people describe while on a GLP-1

Body image and identity

Your body can change faster than your sense of who you are. Some people feel unsure about the person they see in the mirror, notice that others treat them differently, or become preoccupied with weight numbers. Clinicians also describe body dysmorphia and new sources of stigma, even for people whose health is improving.

A changed relationship with food

If food has been how you cope, celebrate, or connect with others, a medication that quiets cravings can leave a gap. Some people feel relief. Others feel a kind of grief for the comfort or pleasure food used to bring, and they need new ways to handle stress, boredom, or loneliness.

Eating disorders and restriction

For people with a history of anorexia or bulimia, these medications may worsen restriction, especially when they are obtained through loosely regulated telehealth services or med spas, according to reporting from the American Psychological Association. If you have ever struggled with disordered eating, tell your prescriber before you start, and consider having a therapist on your team.

Relationships

Partners, family, and friends may comment on your body, change how they eat around you, or feel uneasy about the changes. Couples and families often need support adjusting together.

Stopping the medication

A 2024 study in JAMA Network Open found that about half of people without diabetes stopped taking a GLP-1 within a year. Reasons included stomach side effects and cost. Stopping can bring up fear of regain, frustration, and shame, all of which are worth talking through.

Can GLP-1 medications affect your mood?

The honest answer is that the research is still mixed. A 2024 study in Scientific Reports found that people with obesity on GLP-1 medications had slightly higher anxiety and suicidal behavior risk, and nearly double the risk of major depression, compared with a control group. A 2024 JAMA Internal Medicine study, funded by Novo Nordisk, found no increase in depression or suicidal thoughts in people without known psychiatric conditions, and a small reduction in depressive symptoms. The FDA and the European Medicines Agency have reviewed the question of suicide risk and deemed the medications safe for now.

What this means for you: your experience matters more than an average. If you notice changes in your mood, energy, or enjoyment after starting a GLP-1 or changing your dose, tell your prescriber and consider talking with a mental health professional.

How therapy can help while you are on a GLP-1

Therapy is not about judging your choice to take a medication. It is a place to understand what the changes mean for you. Depending on your goals, therapy can help you:

  • Notice and work through anxiety, low mood, or other changes you are experiencing.
  • Make sense of body image shifts and an identity that is changing.
  • Grieve what food used to give you, and build new ways to cope and find enjoyment.
  • Build a steadier relationship with food and habits that you can sustain.
  • Work through relationship changes with a partner, family, or friends.
  • Process the way past experiences, including trauma, may be tied to eating and your body.

This is the heart of my GLP-1 and weight loss therapy. My approach is warm and collaborative, and I draw from evidence-based approaches including Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT). I do not prescribe or give nutrition advice, so I work alongside the medical team supporting you.

Therapist or prescriber: who should you call?

Call your prescriber about persistent nausea or vomiting, severe stomach pain, signs of dehydration, or any new or worsening physical symptom. Also tell them about mood changes that start after you begin or change a dose.

Consider a therapist if you feel anxious, low, or stuck about your body or food, if your sense of identity feels shaky, if relationships are strained, or if you want support building habits that last. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 right away.

Frequently asked questions

Can GLP-1 medications like Ozempic, Wegovy, or Zepbound affect your mood?

Research so far is mixed. A 2024 study in Scientific Reports found that people with obesity taking GLP-1 medications had slightly higher anxiety and suicidal behavior risk, and nearly double the risk of major depression, compared with a control group. A 2024 JAMA Internal Medicine study, funded by the drug maker Novo Nordisk, found no increase in depression or suicidal thoughts among people without known psychiatric conditions, and a small reduction in depressive symptoms. The FDA and the European Medicines Agency have reviewed the question of suicide risk and deemed the medications safe for now. If you notice changes in your mood after starting a GLP-1 or changing a dose, tell your prescriber and consider talking with a mental health professional.

Do I need therapy to get a GLP-1 prescription?

Not in general, but requirements vary by prescriber and insurance plan. Some plans now ask patients to take part in a lifestyle or behavioral program before or while covering these medications for weight loss. Ask your insurer and your prescriber what your plan requires. Therapy for the emotional side of GLP-1 use is separate from any program a plan may require, and I cannot promise that a plan will count it.

What is food noise, and what happens when it goes quiet?

People often use the phrase food noise to describe near-constant thoughts about food, cravings, and what to eat next. Some people on GLP-1 medications say that noise goes quiet, which can be a relief. For others, the quiet also brings up feelings that food was covering, such as stress, loneliness, or boredom. Therapy can help you understand those feelings and build other ways to cope with them.

Can therapy help if I have stopped taking a GLP-1 medication?

Yes. A 2024 study in JAMA Network Open found that about half of people without diabetes stopped taking a GLP-1 within a year. Stopping can bring up worry about regaining weight, frustration, or shame. Therapy gives you a place to work through those feelings and to build habits and self-trust that do not depend on any one medication.

Can you prescribe medication or give nutrition advice?

No. I am a licensed therapist, not a prescriber or a dietitian. I focus on the emotional and behavioral side of GLP-1 use and weight loss, such as body image, identity, stress, and your relationship with food. Questions about dosing, side effects, or nutrition belong with your prescriber and your medical team.

Does therapy for GLP-1 support take insurance?

I accept Blue Cross Blue Shield of Nebraska and private pay. Whether a plan covers sessions depends on your plan and your diagnosis, so it is worth checking your benefits or asking me during a free 15-minute consultation.

Do you offer telehealth therapy in Nebraska?

Yes. I currently offer telehealth sessions only, so you can meet from home through secure video. I work with women in Nebraska, including Omaha, Lincoln, and smaller towns across the state.
